# Research the market, then write and structure the site

Before styling a real client's site, understand the customer, offer, market and intended action. The agent does the research and proposes a direction; the person supplies business truth and chooses what fits. Keep this proportional: a focused first pass should lead to a draft, not weeks of research before a local business can launch.

## 1. Frame the business

Establish the service, actual service area, ideal customer, primary enquiry/action, differentiators the owner can substantiate, and constraints such as budget, availability or existing brand. Ask which competitors customers mention, then verify the market independently. Save agreed facts and unanswered questions in private `.agency-commons/BRIEF.md`.

For a new idea, identify the customer's current alternatives as well as businesses with similar websites. Doing it themselves, waiting, or choosing a different service may be more relevant than a visually similar brand.

## 2. Identify useful competitors and references

Research a manageable initial set, usually three to five direct competitors and one or two useful reference sites. Adjust for the actual market; don't invent competitors to fill a quota.

| Group | Why include it | How to vet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct local competitors | Same customer, service and geography | Confirm current service and area on the business's own site; check relevant local listings and observed search visibility |
| Close competitors or substitutes | Similar customer need, price range or buying decision | Explain the meaningful overlap and difference; don't equate a national chain with a sole trader without context |
| Strong reference businesses | Useful content, trust or interaction patterns from another market | Name the specific pattern worth studying; label these as references rather than local competitors |

Search service-plus-location and relevant customer questions. Record query, location if known, date, and source URL. Separate paid listings, map/local results and organic results. Search results vary by location and context: “top” needs a stated basis, not a universal leaderboard. If local results can't be verified, say so and combine the available evidence with the owner's knowledge.

Use web/search tools for discovery and a browser tool for rendered pages and interactions. A text fetch cannot establish what is above the fold. Inspect public pages without submitting competitor lead forms or contacting their staff. Don't require paid SEO tools for this step; unknown traffic/revenue stays unknown.

## 3. Record what is visible and what remains a hypothesis

Create private `.agency-commons/RESEARCH.md` with this compact table:

| Competitor / URL / date | Why relevant | Offer and primary CTA | First screen and section order | Trust evidence and friction | What we would adapt / reject | Evidence strength |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fill from live research | Specific market overlap | Observed wording, paraphrased | Actual mobile/desktop observations | Verified proof or unsupported claims | Reason tied to our customer | Observation / hypothesis / documented outcome |

Assess the service pages, navigation, contact/booking path, form fields, FAQs, useful articles, local details, photography and proof. Look for clear differentiation and unanswered customer questions, not just visual polish. Reviews may reveal recurring customer concerns, but selected reviews don't prove a site's design caused business success.

**A competitor having a pattern does not prove it has been tested or works.** Repeated patterns suggest conventions worth considering. Public case studies with a baseline, method, dates and measured outcome provide stronger evidence, but their audience and traffic may differ. Ad longevity, search visibility, review volume and a successful-looking brand are indirect signals; none reveals conversion rate by itself. Mark inferences explicitly.

Recommend a few patterns to adapt, explain why, and list what evidence would change the decision. Do not copy distinctive text, images, testimonials, branding or business claims. A client cannot inherit a competitor's credentials or guarantees.

## 4. Write the offer and copy before decorating

Build a short message brief: who the service is for, the problem/desired result, what is actually offered, why this business is a credible choice, likely objections, and the next step. Collect authentic customer language from authorized notes and owner interviews; paraphrase external research rather than copying it.

Produce a small number of headline/CTA directions when the choice matters. Judge clarity and relevance instead of cleverness alone. For example, a fictional cleaning business might start with “Home cleaning in [verified service area]” and “Request a cleaning quote.” Add a specific benefit only when the owner can substantiate it. Don't invent turnaround times, awards, savings, urgency, guarantees or review counts to strengthen the copy.

Each section should answer a customer question or support a decision. Use concrete language, useful headings, specific services, honest proof, relevant objections and an accurate action label. State what happens after a request when that process is known. Check that every prominent claim has an owner/source and every CTA leads to the promised action.

## 5. Plan the site and each page

Create private `.agency-commons/SITE-PLAN.md`: page URL, intended customer/question, primary action, required facts/proof, inbound/outbound links and section outline. Start with one page when it adequately answers the brief. Add service pages for distinct needs, real location information where useful, an about/team page when it builds credible trust, and blog articles that answer supporting questions. Don't generate near-identical location pages.

An initial homepage outline might be:

1. Clear offer, intended customer/location, primary action, and relevant supporting visual.
2. Real proof or a concise reason to trust the business, when available.
3. Service scope and customer outcomes.
4. Process and what happens next.
5. Relevant work examples or substantiated differentiators.
6. Important objections, practical details and FAQs.
7. A useful contact or booking opportunity.

This is a starting hypothesis, not a required template. An emergency service may prioritize a phone action and coverage immediately. A high-consideration design service may need work examples before an enquiry. Paid-ad landing pages should match the ad's actual offer and audience rather than sending every visitor to a generic homepage.

## 6. Make the first screen useful

“Above the fold” means the content initially visible in that visitor's viewport; it isn't a fixed pixel height. Inspect real phone and desktop sizes. The opening should help a visitor understand what the business offers, whether it is relevant, and what to do next. Keep navigation clear and avoid oversized decoration burying the message. Don't force every detail into the first screen or assume people never scroll.

[Nielsen Norman Group's scrolling research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) found attention concentrated toward the top while people also scrolled. It is a dated desktop study, not a conversion guarantee or a universal percentage to apply to a local mobile audience. Use it as context for prioritization, then check the actual page and audience.

Show the first screen briefly to someone unfamiliar with the business and ask them what is offered, who it is for, and what they would do next. Treat that as a small usability observation, not a statistically validated experiment. Record their actual response, repair confusion, and continue.

## 7. Learn from this site's results

After launch, verify measurement before interpreting it. Follow [GROWTH.md](GROWTH.md) to distinguish visits, contact clicks, saved enquiries and qualified leads. Keep a dated change log with the hypothesis, change, outcome and caveats. Many local sites lack enough traffic for reliable A/B tests; use customer feedback and clear usability failures without pretending a small before/after change proves causality.

[Google's helpful-content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) favors useful original information and added value over simply rewriting other sources. Let research reveal customer needs, then use the client's expertise to answer them. Follow [BLOG.md](BLOG.md) for ongoing content and [FRONTEND.md](FRONTEND.md) for implementation.
